Western Art Mini‑Wiki · New Lecture Set

This mini‑wiki condenses your recent lectures: The Human Body, The Artist & Patronage, Social & Historical Contexts, Gender & Ethnicity, and Rethinking Art in the 21st Century. It is written in exam‑oriented language.

How to Use This File Exam

  • Use the sidebar to jump by theme:
    • Body & nudity, gender and race, patronage and art market, modern city & class, digital/immersive art.
  • Each page pulls together:
    • Key concepts and definitions (good for Section A questions).
    • Representative artworks with interpretation (good for Section B essays).

The Human Body 1: Naked vs Nude & Classical Canon

Key Distinction: Naked vs Nude

  • Naked
    • Simply without clothes; an unidealized individual body.
    • Linked to mundane reality, exposure, vulnerability.
    • Connotations: embarrassment, shame, abnormality.
  • Nude (term gains its art‑historical meaning in 18th c.)
    • A balanced, perfect, “pure” condition.
    • Idealized, depersonalized image = a standard of beauty, not a specific person.
    • Connotations: pride, confidence, pleasure.
  • The nude as an art form is invented in Classical Greece (5th c. BC) with the Classical Canon.

Early Greek Pride in the Male Body

Minoan & Pre‑classical Background

  • Fisherman with his Catch, c. 1600–1500 BC, Minoan fresco, Santorini.
    • Shows an early male nude.
    • Indicates that Greek culture valued the male body even before the Classical period.
  • Ancient Greek attitudes:
    • Pride in physical beauty; gymnasium culture.
    • Plato: love between young men is “nobler” and more natural than heterosexual love → helps explain focus on the ideal male body.

The Classical Canon of the Male Nude

Doryphoros (Spear Bearer)

  • Doryphoros (Spear Carrier), Polykleitos, 450–440 BC, Roman marble copy of Greek bronze.
  • Embodies the Classical Canon:
    • Contrapposto: weight on one leg, balanced tilt of hips and shoulders.
    • Harmony and proportion (e.g. head ≈ 1/8 of total height).
    • Organic, naturalistic, yet idealized: a figure at rest but “ready for action”.
  • Underlying idea: beauty of the body reflects goodness of the soul; physical perfection implies moral and divine qualities.

Discobolus (Discus Thrower)

  • Discobolus, 460–450 BC, Roman marble copy.
  • Shows the athletic version of the ideal male nude:
    • Restrained dynamism: movement is frozen at a balanced moment.
    • Smooth, polished outline → artificial, ideal beauty, not a real athlete.

Vitruvian Man & Proportion

  • Vitruvian Man, Leonardo da Vinci, 1485–90, ink on paper.
    • Illustrates Vitruvius’s theory in De Architectura (Book III).
    • Body inscribed in circle and square → microcosm of the universe.
    • Renaissance re‑uses Classical ideas: mathematics & geometry explain bodily beauty.
  • Not all Renaissance scholars produced satisfactory illustrations; Leonardo’s version becomes the standard.

Androgynous Ideal

  • Death of Joseph Bara, David, c. 1794.
    • French Neoclassicism → androgyny (boyish, soft body) as symbol of purity.
    • Fourteen‑year‑old revolutionary martyr: youthful beauty = political virtue.
  • Classical male nude remains the aesthetic ideal (heroes, athletes, gods) until the 17th c.; by the 19th c. it is largely replaced by the female nude.

The Human Body 2: Venus & the Female Nude

From Fertility Figurines to Classical Venus

Prehistoric “Venus” Figures

  • Venus of Hohle Fels (ivory), Venus of Dolní Věstonice (ceramic), Venus of Willendorf (limestone), c. 35,000–24,000 BC.
  • Exaggerated breasts, belly, hips → fertility and abundance.
  • Show that female nudity linked to fertility cults across prehistoric Europe.

Greek Women Before Venus

  • Kore, c. 520 BC, Athenian Acropolis:
    • Always clothed; garments = modesty and domestic role.
  • Caryatids, Erechtheion, Acropolis, 5th c. BC:
    • Female figures used as architectural supports.
    • Commemorate defeat of Caryae → symbol of humiliation, though stylistically naturalistic.

Birth of the Nude Goddess

  • Ancient Greek women had subordinate status; full female nudity appears only from the 4th c. BC.
  • Influence from Near Eastern / Oriental cults.
  • Aphrodite/Venus = first fully nude goddess; becomes generic term for ideal female nudity.
  • Plato: two Venuses:
    • Celestial Venus – spiritual, ideal beauty.
    • Vulgar Venus – physical, object of desire.

Classical & Roman Venus Types

Wet Drapery & Venus Pudica

  • Aphrodite of Fréjus (Venus Genetrix), Roman copy:
    • Wet drapery clings to the body, revealing rather than covering.
    • Heightens eroticism while keeping a “respectable” mythological context.
  • Cnidian Aphrodite (Praxiteles), Roman copy:
    • First life‑size female nude in Greek art.
    • Uses Classical canon: 8 heads high, contrapposto.
    • Venus pudica type: gesture of covering genitals while being seen → both modest and erotic.
  • Capitoline Venus, Roman copy:
    • Forward‑bending, self‑protective pose intensifies both defence and sexuality.
    • Awareness of “intruder” = male viewer/voyeur.
    • Sits between nudity and nakedness; ideal yet exposed.

Roman Reclining Venus

  • Reclining Venus, House of Venus, Pompeii, wall painting:
    • Scene of Venus’s birth; reclining type that later Renaissance art revives.
    • Shawl billows like drapery; emphasizes divine love yet also sensual form.

Renaissance Re‑spiritualisation & Re‑eroticisation

Botticelli’s Spiritual Venus

  • Birth of Venus, 1483–85; Venus, c. 1482.
    • Early Renaissance attempt to capture spiritual, ideal female beauty.
    • Linked to Medici circle discussions of Platonic love (spiritual vs physical).
    • Girlish innocence, dreamy expression, other‑worldliness.

Venetian Colour & Sensuality

  • Sacred and Profane Love, Titian, 1515:
    • Two women as terrestrial (profane) and celestial (sacred) Venus.
    • Uses allegory of love as marriage gift: sacred love is “higher”, but profane love addresses viewer directly.
  • Sleeping Venus, c. 1510:
    • Early reclining Venus type → man and nature in harmony.
    • Unites terrestrial and celestial Venus.
  • Venus of Urbino, 1538:
    • Now clearly a courtesan, not a goddess.
    • Direct gaze and relaxed nudity = seductive, inviting.
    • Indicates rise of eroticism in high art.

From “High Art Taste” to Explicit Eroticism

  • Tribuna of the Uffizi, 1772–78:
    • Shows Venus sculptures as objects of desire in a male‑dominated art market.
    • Eroticism disguised as “good taste” among elites.
  • Baroque Venus – Rubens’s The Fur (Helena Fourment as Venus):
    • Real, fleshy woman, sensual and worldly.
    • Direct message: pleasure of life, not just ideal philosophy.

Modern Challenges to Classical Beauty

  • Olympia, Manet, 1863:
    • Prostitute, confrontational stare; hand firmly covers genitals → ownership of her sexuality.
    • Flat, pale, unidealized flesh challenges academic Venus standards.
    • Critics: “bad art”, but important for modern notion of the modern woman.
  • The Birth of Venus, Cabanel, 1863:
    • Salon success; submissive, welcoming nude, idealized and decorative.
    • Reveals hypocritical taste of Parisian high society (sexual appeal masked as myth).
  • Mary Magdalene in the Grotto, 1876:
    • Religious subject but nude is defenceless and eroticized – sinner as sexual object.
  • Dryad (Nude in the Forest), Picasso, 1908:
    • Proto‑Cubism, African influence, primitivism.
    • Primordial woman: crude, distorted, replacing Classical beauty.
    • Challenges Eurocentric beauty standards.
  • Branded, Jenny Saville, 1992:
    • Monumental self‑portrait; body enlarged, marked, almost monstrous.
    • Attacks unhealthy contemporary ideal of the female body.
    • Uses ugliness and scale to critique “idealization of self”.

The Human Body 3: Christian Morality & the Body

Medieval Suspicion of the Body

  • Middle Ages: nudity generally forbidden, linked to sin, shame, humiliation.
  • Body seen as dangerously sensual; excessive pleasure leads to destruction.

Nudity as Sin, Shame & Punishment

  • Temptation of Eve, 1120–1132, St. Lazare, Autun:
    • Nudity associated with original sin.
  • Adam’s Portal, c. 1235, Bamberg Cathedral:
    • Nude figures = martyrdom, torture, humiliation.
    • Sensuality minimized; forms are unnatural, unrealistic.
  • Last Judgment, Giotto, 1303–06, Scrovegni Chapel:
    • Saved: clothed, welcomed to Paradise.
    • Damned: naked, cast into Hell.
  • Detail of Devil and the Damned:
    • Nudity has two meanings:
      • Bestial – demonic, animal side (Devil).
      • Vulnerability – human weakness and exposure.
  • Inferno, c. 1410, San Petronio, Bologna:
    • Reinforces moral message: body’s pleasures are dangerous and must be controlled.

From Spiritual to Erotic

  • Renaissance:
    • Revives Classical nude; shifts from purely spiritual symbolism to increasing eroticism.
    • Mythological wrapping (“this is Venus”) is used to sanitize erotic content.
  • Baroque & 17th c. onwards:
    • Real, fleshy women disguised as Venuses.
    • Salon system and art market are male‑dominated – erotic female nude becomes profitable “high art”.
  • Impressionism:
    • Marks beginning of the modern woman – Olympia’s agency and self‑possession.
  • 20th century:
    • Feminist critiques re‑evaluate the nude as site of objectification, control, and resistance.

The Artist & Patronage 1: Status of the Artist

From Plato’s “Double Falsity” to Low Status

  • Plato devalues art:
    • Visible world already an imitation of true Ideas.
    • Art imitates this imitation → “imitation of an imitation”.
    • Art leads us further away from truth.
  • Result: artist holds a low social status from antiquity into Middle Ages.

Classical & Medieval Periods: Artist as Labourer

Classical Period

  • Visual arts & handicrafts = mechanical arts, not liberal (intellectual) arts.
  • Artists = labourers, low status.
  • Main patron = the state (public sculpture, temples, etc.).

Medieval Period

  • Art seen as service to God.
  • Artists treated as handworkers; rarely glorified individually.
  • Chief patrons: Church, papacy, aristocrats.

Anonymous Craftsmen & Rare Signatures

  • François Vase, c. 560 BC, black‑figure krater:
    • In ancient Greece, potter’s craftsmanship valued more than painter’s.
    • Potter usually signed; painter remained anonymous.
  • Mourning Scene, end 5th c. BC, white‑ground lekythos:
    • Painters seldom sign; this one known only by favourite motif.
  • Last Judgment, St. Lazare, Autun, 1120–1132:
    • Sculptor Gislebertus signs “Gislebertus hoc fecit” – rare assertion of authorship.

Renaissance Humanism: Artist as Intellectual & Celebrity

  • Renaissance = turning point:
    • Artists seen as intellectuals, geniuses (not mere craftsmen).
    • Giorgio Vasari’s Lives of the Artists (1550/1568) → first systematic biographies.
    • Rise of secular patronage: rich families commission famous artists to project power.
  • Perugino, Delivery of the Keys, 1482:
    • Italian Renaissance painter called by Pope to fresco Sistine Chapel.
    • Self‑portrait in crowd → self‑consciousness of artist’s cultural importance.
  • Raphael, La Fornarina, 1518–19:
    • Signature “Raphael Vrbinas” on armband, in Latin.
    • Latin = claim to status as Classical scholar and humanist.
  • Dürer, Self‑Portrait in Fur, 1500:
    • Full frontal pose reserved for Christ → elevates himself to quasi‑sacred status.
    • Type recalls Salvator Mundi; cross‑like structure leads eye to his hand and gaze.
    • Example of artist as early celebrity in German Renaissance.

The Artist & Patronage 2: Church, Court & Merchant Patronage

Church & Papal Patronage

  • Renaissance & Baroque: Catholic Church remains a major patron:
    • Commission large fresco cycles, altarpieces, architecture.
    • Counter‑Reformation (17–18th c.) pushes for emotionally persuasive art.
  • Masaccio, Trinity, 1426–28:
    • Painted chapel with perfect linear perspective.
    • Includes donor couple (Domenico Lenzi and wife?) kneeling – early donor portrait.
  • Portinari Altarpiece, c. 1475:
    • Nativity scene with donors Tommaso Portinari, his wife Maria Baroncelli, and children.
    • Uses hierarchy of size (medieval convention) rather than strict realism.

Merchant Families & Display of Wealth

Florence: Strozzi & Medici Rivalry

  • Adoration of the Magi, Gentile da Fabriano, 1423:
    • Lavish Gothic altarpiece commissioned by wealthy merchant Palla Strozzi.
    • Imaginative, exotic costumes & extravagance display his wealth.
    • Strozzi and son appear just behind the third king.
  • Procession of the Magi, Benozzo Gozzoli, c. 1459–61, Medici‑Riccardi Palace chapel:
    • Members of Medici family and painter’s self‑portrait inserted into sacred procession.
    • Signatures such as “Opus Benotii” claim authorship.
  • Botticelli, Adoration of the Magi, c. 1475:
    • Not officially Medici‑commissioned, but includes Medici portraits anyway → favourable association.
    • Botticelli includes his own self‑portrait.

Court Artists & Royal Patronage

  • 17th–18th c.: court artists gain international fame; royal academies formalise artistic training.
  • Key institutions:
    • French Royal Academy of Painting and Sculpture (1648).
    • British Royal Academy of Arts (1768).
  • Rubens, Self‑Portrait with Isabella Brant, 1609–10:
    • Flemish Baroque painter with over 100 assistants.
    • Presents himself as a refined gentleman (sword, orange stockings); celebrates his marriage into high society.
  • Velázquez:
    • Self‑portrait, 1639: most sought‑after painter in Europe, also a diplomat.
    • Las Meninas, 1656:
      • Combines royal portrait with artist’s self‑representation in studio.
      • Reflection of royal couple in mirror + painter at work emphasise his closeness to power.
      • Granted knighthood in 1658 by Philip IV → artist gains noble status.

The Artist & Patronage 3: Modern Market & Supercollectors

19th Century: Independence & Art Dealers

  • Cheaper, widely available art materials → more people become artists.
  • Art dealers begin to replace traditional patronage:
    • Artists become more independent but also precarious (Bohemian lifestyle).
    • Must compete in a crowded, market‑driven system.
  • Van Gogh, The Red Vineyards of Arles, 1888:
    • Produced over 2000 works but sold only one painting in his lifetime.
    • Highlights the gap between later fame and contemporary poverty.

Early 20th Century: Dealers, Critics & Modernism

  • Picasso, La Vie, 1903:
    • Blue Period; expresses misery (hunger, cold, death of friend) while trying to establish himself in Paris.
  • Art critics and dealers increasingly shape reputations and value.

Collectors, Museums & New Patronage

  • Vasari, Lives of the Artists:
    • Creates a canon of great artists from Cimabue to mid‑16th c.
    • Helps justify collecting and museum display.
  • Uffizi, Florence:
    • Begun 1560 as Medici office and art collection; prototype of art museum.
  • Guggenheim Museums:
    • Solomon R. Guggenheim (collection 1937) → New York museum (1957–59) for modern art.
    • Peggy Guggenheim Collection, Venice (1951) – important for Surrealism and Abstract Expressionism.
    • Guggenheim Bilbao (1997); Guggenheim Abu Dhabi (planned 2026) → global network, merging architecture, tourism, and art.

Abstract Expressionism & the Power of Patron + Critic

  • Jackson Pollock, Mural, 1943:
    • Commissioned by Peggy Guggenheim for her New York home.
    • Catalyst for his move into large, all‑over abstraction.
  • Clement Greenberg, critic:
    • Hails Pollock as “greatest painter of the age”.
    • Defines Modernism as art that emphasizes abstraction and flatness.
  • Photo of Pollock in studio, 1950:
    • Media images turn him into a celebrity, despite alcoholism and troubled personal life.

Late 20th Century: Supercollectors & Shock Art

  • Collectors like Charles Saatchi become new patrons:
    • Support Young British Artists (YBA) in 1990s.
    • Use marketing, sensationalism, and media to boost value.
  • Damien Hirst, The Physical Impossibility of Death in the Mind of Someone Living, 1991:
    • Shark in formaldehyde tank.
    • Saatchi gives Hirst absolute freedom.
    • Work becomes centrepiece of YBA show (1992); Sun newspaper headline: “£50,000 for Fish without Chips”.
    • Contemporary memento mori; also example of art as high‑value commodity.
  • Tracey Emin, My Bed, 1998:
    • Autobiographical installation of unmade bed, bottles, cigarettes, underwear.
    • Saatchi outbids Tate and buys it for £150,000.
    • Emin becomes celebrity artist; later model for Vivienne Westwood, Turner Prize nominee, Venice Biennale representative.
  • Tracey Emin, I’ve Got It All, 2000:
    • Self‑image with notes of money pouring out of her lap; mocks symbols of success with vulgarity.

Questions of Value in the Contemporary Market

  • Before Renaissance: value = largely material cost (gold, pigments, size).
  • Since Renaissance: payment also for creativity & skill.
  • Modern avant‑garde fully liberates art from old rules:
    • Anything can be art (shark, bed, banana with duct tape).
  • With rise of supercollectors in late 20th/21st c.:
    • Who and what determines value – artist, critic, collector, auction house, media?
    • What strategies (shock, scale, scarcity, branding) are used to boost value?
    • Can such value last, or is it fashion‑driven?
    • What impact will AI have on authorship, creativity and value?

Social & Historical Contexts: 18–19C English & French Painting

England: Civil War, Empire & Civic Consciousness

  • English Civil Wars (1642–51) → execution of Charles I (1649) → end of absolute monarchy.
  • Parliament of Great Britain (1707) gains control → partial democracy.
  • British Empire expands via colonialism; frequent conflicts with France.
  • These contexts foster a strong civic consciousness in English painters and unique moral themes.

William Hogarth & Modern Moral Subjects

  • Execution of Charles I, c. 1649–50, history context for later political awareness.
  • Election Series: Canvassing for Votes, 1754–55:
    • Series of four paintings about corruption in early democratic system.
    • Modern moral subject: electoral fraud in notorious Oxfordshire election of 1754.
    • Painter acts as satirist and social critic.
  • Marriage à la Mode I–VI, 1743–45:
    • Another modern moral subject – satire on marriage of convenience.
    • Arranged marriage between aristocrat’s son and rich merchant’s daughter.
    • Exposes hypocrisy and moral decay of upper classes.

Empire, Colonial Subjects & Native Allies

  • George Clive and his Family with an Indian Maid, c. 1765:
    • Family portrait with Indian maid signifying colonial possession of India.
    • Connects to British East India Company rule from 1757.
  • Colonel Guy Johnson and Karonghyontye (Captain David Hill), c. 1775:
    • British officer with Mohawk chief advisor.
    • Painting suggests that peace and order are guaranteed by British rule → imperial ideology.
  • Death of General Wolfe, 1770:
    • Modern history painting (near‑contemporary event – conquest of Quebec, 1759).
    • Heroes in contemporary dress, but with classical poses (Wolfe reminiscent of Christ).
    • Tattooed Native American in contemplative pose symbolises noble savage idea.

Victorian Morality & Gender Double Standards

  • Queen Victoria (1837–1901):
    • Upholds Protestant Puritanism.
    • Emphasis on individual religious, social, and moral duties.
    • High, strict moral standard for all; sexual repression but strong gender inequality.
    • Nude art widely condemned.

Augustus Egg, Past and Present I–III (1858)

  • Triptych on consequences of female adultery:
    • I: Husband discovers wife’s affair.
    • II: Five years later – father dead, mother expelled, two orphan girls left.
    • III: Wife, with illegitimate child, homeless under London arch, full of regret.
  • Comment from Frances Fowle: in Victorian England, men could safely keep mistresses, but female infidelity was “unforgivable crime”.

Pre‑Raphaelites: Moral Allegory & Individual Responsibility

  • Awakening Conscience, 1853:
    • Inspired by Dickens’s David Copperfield.
    • Young mistress kept by gentleman has sudden spiritual awakening.
    • Mirror = lost innocence; ray of light on her = possibility of redemption.
    • Loaded with symbolic objects (music, furnishings, etc.).
  • Light of the World, 1853:
    • Christ knocking on unopened door → human soul.
    • Emphasizes Protestant idea that duty to God is an individual responsibility.
  • Laus Veneris (Praise of Venus), 1873–78:
    • Dreamy, remote, pale women → Victorian beauty as chaste yet object of desire.
    • Borrow Botticelli’s ivory flesh and linear elegance.

France: Haussmann’s Paris, Modernity & Alienation

Haussmannization

  • Napoleon III commissions Baron Haussmann to rebuild Paris (from 1850s):
    • New grands boulevards; connect centre to periphery.
    • City divided into arrondissements (12, later 20) → modern management system.
    • Vistas and visual unity: straight roads to monuments; uniform building heights (~20 m).
    • Public health: sewers, gas pipes, gas lamps.
    • Political aim: wide streets prevent barricades and ease troop movement.
  • Photographs show old Paris as overcrowded, unsanitary, polluted.

Impressionist Urban Scenes

  • Boulevard des Capucines, Monet, 1873–74:
    • Depicts one of the grands boulevards widened in 19th c.
    • Shows modern city as social space of leisure and movement.
  • Paris Opéra House, 1862–75:
    • Symbol of Paris as city of luxury.
    • Sumptuous theatre for entertainment of the rich.
  • Renoir, The Loge, 1882:
    • Focus on theatre interior; interest in artificial light and reflection.
    • Reveals glamour and modernity of Parisian high society.
  • Paris Street; Rainy Day, Caillebotte, 1877:
    • Wide Haussmannian crossroads with gas streetlights.
    • Well‑dressed bourgeois couple isolated in modern crowd → urban alienation.

Social Costs: Displacement & Misery

  • Haussmann’s project demolished ~20,000 buildings; built ~35,000 new ones:
    • Medieval dwellings replaced by expensive boulevards and apartments.
    • Poor residents pushed to periphery → social fragmentation.
  • Third‑Class Carriage, Daumier, c. 1862:
    • Shows lower‑class passengers in cramped train compartment.
    • French Realism; critiques consequences of modernization.

Alienation, Absinthe & Nightlife

  • In a Café (L’Absinthe), Degas, 1875–76:
    • Captures psychological state of degraded city dwellers (actress/prostitute, artist/bohemian).
    • Sense of isolation despite crowded modern city.
  • The Absinthe Drinker, Manet, 1858–59:
    • Vagabond ragpicker portrayed with monumental dignity.
    • Ironically dressed like dandy; reveals “other side” of Paris – city of misery.
  • Van Gogh portrait drinking absinthe, 1887:
    • Absinthe as cheap drink popular among bohemian artists and writers; symbol of escapism and decadence.
  • Toulouse‑Lautrec:
    • Crippled aristocrat rejected by high society; lives among café‑concerts and brothels.
    • Post‑Impressionist; dies at 36 from alcoholism and syphilis.
    • Posters (Moulin Rouge: La Goulue, Divan Japonais) blur line between high art and advertising.
    • Paintings like At the Moulin Rouge, In the Salon of Rue des Moulins, and Rue des Moulins – The Medical Inspection reveal backstage world of sex work, boredom, medical control.

Gender & Ethnicity 1: Gender Stereotypes & the Male Gaze

Postmodern Turn: Gender & Race in Art

  • From 1960s, post‑modern culture raises attention to:
    • Gender, race, violence, war, poverty, AIDS, environment.
  • Gender and racial discrimination in Western art are critically examined.
  • Rise of Feminist art and discussions about race and ethnicity.

Woman as “Other” in Western Art

Machismo & the Heroic Male

  • Oath of the Horatii, David, 1784:
    • Exaggerates value of masculine heroism and duty.
    • Women depicted as emotional, passive, secondary.
    • Example of gender stereotyping: woman as the “other”, not the universal subject.

Women as Objects of Male Desire

  • Brenn and his Share of the Plunder, 1893 (French academic history painting):
    • Nominally about Gauls occupying Rome (4th c. BC).
    • Real subject: eroticism, voyeurism, fetishism.
    • Women displayed as prey and spoils for male spectator.
  • Naked (or Nude) Maja, Goya, c. 1795–1800:
    • Spanish Romanticism.
    • Projects male desire: sinuous, libertine beauty “ready for consumption”.
    • Direct eye contact like Titian’s Venus of Urbino; BBC comment: “Come and get me, boys!”.

Orientalism & Exoticised Female Bodies

  • Turkish Bath, Ingres, 1862:
    • French Neoclassicism.
    • Using “Othering” strategy: eroticism made acceptable via exotic harem setting.
    • Intended audience: white European men.
  • La Grande Odalisque, Ingres, 1814:
    • European slave in Oriental harem.
    • Orientalism (Edward Said, 1978): stereotyped, distorted Western view of Arab/Asian cultures.

Gender & Ethnicity 2: Feminist Art & Reclaiming the Female Image

Feminist Critique of the Gaze

  • Barbara Kruger, Untitled (Your Gaze Hits the Side of My Face), 1981:
    • Photograph + bold text; aggressive slogan over magazine photo.
    • Points to woman as victim of objectification.
    • Language of advertising turned into critique.
  • Brigitte Cornand, The Turkish Bath, 1973:
    • Reverses tradition of male artist contemplating nude women.
    • Raises question: is the female gaze replacing the male gaze in popular culture?

Guerrilla Girls & Institutional Critique

  • Guerrilla Girls, Untitled (Do Women Have to Be Naked to Get into the Met. Museum?), 1985–90:
    • Poster: points out that women are mostly present in museums as nudes, not as artists.
    • Highlights twofold discrimination:
      • Women’s bodies idealized and objectified.
      • Women artists seldom recognized.
  • The Advantages of Being a Woman Artist, 1988:
    • List format; sarcastic “advantages” expose structural inequality (e.g. less pressure because low expectations).

Body Image, Kitsch & Banality

  • Pop & Post‑Pop: Koons
    • Michael Jackson and Bubbles, 1988; Pink Panther, 1988:
      • Highly polished porcelain/ceramic sculptures from Banality series.
      • Critique of kitsch – beauty of mass‑produced commodities, decorative, without deep aesthetic reflection.
      • Koons urges bourgeois viewers to drop guilt about banality.
      • Critics say he appropriates mass‑culture objects without critical distance.
  • Niki de Saint Phalle, Black Nana, 1968–69:
    • Painted polyester; colourful, disproportioned “Nana”.
    • Claims that obese, colour‑skinned, disproportioned women can be happy and beautiful.
    • Artist’s trauma and modelling experience lead her to re‑think femininity.
  • Jenny Saville, Hybrid, 1997:
    • Huge oil painting; female body constructed and mythologized for perfect beauty.
    • Women willingly subject themselves to diet, exercise, plastic surgery → social norms hurt the body.
  • Jenny Holzer, Truisms:
    • Light installation, e.g. “Murder has its sexual side”.
    • Simple statements (“truisms”) raise concern over woman as sexual victim.

Family, Trauma & Maternal Power

  • Louise Bourgeois, The Destruction of the Father, 1974:
    • Plaster, latex, wood, fabric, red light: womb‑like installation.
    • Children murder and eat dominant father in fantasy → response to patriarchal family.
  • Femme Maison (Housewife), 1994:
    • Marble figure whose head is a house; woman trapped by domesticity.
    • Alludes to prehistoric Venus of Dolní Věstonice but re‑coded under modern gender analysis.
  • Maman, 1999:
    • Bronze and steel giant spider outside Tate Modern.
    • Homage to her mother; expresses power, protection, strength using traditionally “masculine” medium of monumental sculpture.

Rewriting History & Women’s Contributions

  • Cindy Sherman, Untitled Film Still #3, 1977:
    • Artist plays a fictitious Hollywood‑style housewife: sexually attractive, tidy home, waiting for husband.
    • Mocks stereotyped 1950s femininity; references culture like Revolutionary Road.
  • Judy Chicago, The Dinner Party, 1979:
    • Triangular table (symbol of femininity & equality) with 39 place settings for important women in history.
    • Each plate shaped like female genitalia.
    • Uses ceramics and textiles to challenge hierarchy of “high” vs “low” art.
  • Virginia Woolf Place Setting:
    • Represents each woman with unique motif; emphasises female creativity and overlooked contributions.

Gender & Ethnicity 3: Ethnicity, Orientalism & Primitivism

Historical Background: Segregation & Orientalism

  • US Jim Crow laws (late 19th c.) enforce racial segregation in facilities (schools, housing, transport, etc.).
  • Segregation declared illegal in 1950s–60s but practice continues.
  • Dominique de Menil funds The Image of the Black in Western Art in 1960s.
  • Edward Said, Orientalism (1978):
    • Concept of the “East” as cultural and political construct produced by Western power.

Blacks in European Christian Art

  • Adoration of the Magi, c. 1485–1500, Prado:
    • From 14th c., black figure appears as third, youngest king.
    • Represents “all mankind” paying homage to Christ – but within Eurocentric hierarchy.
    • Exoticised by earrings, unusual clothes.
  • Baptism of the Eunuch, 1626:
    • Popular from 15th–17th c.; St Philip baptizes Ethiopian eunuch.
    • Symbolizes inclusion of blacks into Christian, “civilized” world.

Noble Savage & Hierarchy of Races

  • Portrait of Mai (Omai), c. 1776:
    • Polynesian visitor to England; represented as “noble savage”.
    • Background evokes Arcadian nature.
    • White men = God’s perfect creation; colonized peoples = idealized but inferior primitives.
  • Goya, Uprising of the Second of May, 1808 (painted 1814):
    • Shows Spanish rebels fighting French troops and Moorish cavalry (Mamelukes).
    • Moors stereotyped as fierce and merciless.
  • Marchesa Elena Grimaldi Cattaneo, van Dyck, 1623:
    • Black servant appears as family property, low status.
    • Reflects history of slave trade in Italy and Europe.
  • Olympia, Manet, 1863:
    • White prostitute with black maid bringing flowers.
    • Black woman remains servant, submissive, background; white woman commanding, proud.

Primitivism in Modern Art

  • Gauguin, Ia Orana Maria (Ave Maria), 1891:
    • Paints Tahitian women as Virgin & Child with angel/Magi figure.
    • French colony; Tahiti seen as new Arcadia (innocent, uncorrupted nature).
    • Imposes Christian iconography on local people → cultural appropriation.
  • Matisse, Blue Nude, 1907:
    • Primitivism: draws on “exotic” forms; figure appears clumsy, distorted.
    • Reveals racist prejudice that non‑Western cultures are primitive and bodies “ugly”.
  • Picasso, Dryad (Nude in the Forest), 1908:
    • African‑influenced Proto‑Cubism; modelled on so‑called “savage” sculpture.
    • Primordial woman: crude, distorted; challenges classical beauty and Eurocentrism.
  • Common stereotypes:
    • Non‑white figures placed in secondary/background roles.
    • Depicted as humble, submissive, or excessively fierce and ugly.

Sexual Violence & the Racialised Body

  • Three Young White Men and a Black Woman (Rape of a Negress), 1632:
    • Dutch Baroque art; depicts an actual sexual assault (not just mythic “abduction”).
    • Black woman not idealized; her blackness and “ugliness” exaggerated.
    • Violence and vulgarity “justified” by supposed inferiority of victim.
  • Rape of the Daughters of Leucippus, Rubens, c. 1618:
    • Mythological abduction (Castor & Pollux kidnap Phoebe and Hilaeira).
    • In Western canon, “rape” often aestheticized and eroticized when victims are white and idealized.

Contemporary Responses by Non‑White Artists

  • Chris Ofili (YBA, British‑Nigerian artist):
    • Challenges Blaxploitation imagery – racial stereotyping in popular culture.
    • Asks how non‑white artists can gain recognition without being limited to ethnic themes.
  • The Holy Virgin Mary, 1996:
    • Mixed media with acrylic, oil, collage, glitter, map pins, elephant dung.
    • Parodies Western assumptions about African identity and Catholic iconography.
    • Commercial cut‑outs of buttocks/genitalia used as “angels” – mocking religious imagery.
    • Use of dung called “blasphemous” → controversy reveals ongoing racism and religious sensitivity.
  • Raphael, Alba Madonna, c. 1510 (context):
    • Traditional Madonna fulfils white male desires:
      • Virgin.
      • Mother.
      • Ideal white beauty.
    • Under gender and racial discourse, this ideal is re‑examined as culturally specific, not universal.

Rethinking Art 1: Global Art Market, Media & Value

Record Prices & New Patrons

  • Salvator Mundi, c. 1500, Leonardo (attributed), oil on panel, 65.6 × 45.4 cm:
    • Sold at Christie’s New York in 2017 for ~US$450 million.
    • Buyer: Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman.
    • Symbol of rise of art market in Asia and the Middle East.
    • Martin Kemp defended authenticity in 2019 (lecture “Leonardo’s Salvator Mundi: Scholarship, Science and Skulduggery”).
  • Beeple, Everydays: The First 5000 Days, 2007–2021:
    • Digital collage of 5000 daily pictures (painting + digital art).
    • Sold for nearly US$70 million at Christie’s online auction (March 2021).
    • Marks entry of purely digital art into blue‑chip auction houses.

Key Issues in 21st‑Century Art

  • Mass culture & art market – artworks circulate globally via social media and auctions.
  • Changing relationships among artist, collector, public, society.
  • New media and digital technologies redefine:
    • What counts as original.
    • How we experience art (screen vs physical).
  • Art display extends beyond museums; art “comes alive” in public space, immersive installations, online platforms.
  • Questions:
    • How to evaluate artworks – are they commodities only?
    • What is authenticity and ownership in digital art (NFTs, reproducibility)?

Pop Art & Conceptual Shifts

  • Warhol, Brillo Box, 1964:
    • Silkscreen ink and house paint on plywood, life‑size replicas of supermarket boxes.
    • Mocks everyday commercial products and consumer culture.
    • Art critic Arthur Danto (1965): asks “Why is this object a work of art?”
    • Work prompts philosophical question: What is the nature of art?

Banksy, Media & Market

  • Girl with Balloon, 2002, London graffiti:
    • British street artist, identity hidden (via “Pest Control”).
    • Works on social and political themes, humanitarian concerns (refugees, war victims).
  • Love is in the Bin, 2018:
    • Print of Girl with Balloon partially shredded at Sotheby’s immediately after being auctioned.
    • Marketed as “first artwork created live during an auction”.
    • Price rose from £1.04 million (2018) to £18.5 million (2021).
    • Shows how media spectacle itself creates value.

Maurizio Cattelan & Hype

  • Comedian, 2019, Art Basel Miami:
    • Duct‑taped banana on wall.
    • Echoes economics book The $12 Million Stuffed Shark (Don Thompson) about contemporary art value.
    • Raises question: art object or just taxidermy/fruit? Where is the value (idea, certificate, media attention)?
  • Julian Stallabrass (Contemporary Art, 2020): “What is popular is valuable, and what is valuable is popular.”
  • Is collecting art today pure investment? Is media attention the main key to success?

Sotheby’s “Value of Art” Criteria

Commercial institutions list the following factors:

  • 1. Authenticity
  • 2. Condition
  • 3. Rarity
  • 4. Provenance (ownership history)
  • 5. Historical significance
  • 6. Size
  • 7. Fashion (current taste)
  • 8. Subject matter
  • 9. Medium
  • 10. Quality

Open Questions

  • What really determines the true value of art today?
  • Can market value reflect aesthetic or social value?
  • How will digital technologies and AI reshape:
    • Authorship, creativity and originality.
    • Distribution and access.
    • Long‑term value and preservation.

Rethinking Art 2: Digital Media, Immersive Art & New Museums

Art as Social & Political Action

  • Keith Haring, Ignorance = Fear, Silence = Death, 1989:
    • Poster for ACT UP; raises awareness of AIDS crisis.
    • Shows how street/pop art can be a tool of activism.
  • Banksy, Napalm, 2004:
    • Appropriation of Nick Ut’s “Napalm Girl” (Vietnam War) with Mickey Mouse & McDonald’s figure.
    • Critiques cultural and commercial invasion by American brands.
  • Graffiti Removal, 2008, Leake Street; mural at Royal Courts of Justice, 2025:
    • Street art removed/covered; criticised as suppression of free speech.
    • Shows tension between political activism and institutional control.
  • Invader, Invasion of Hong Kong IV (Pac‑Man Game), 2014:
    • French artist places “Space Invaders” mosaics worldwide.
    • Street art as liberation of art from institutional boundaries, close to everyday life.

Performance, Fluxus & Time‑based Media

  • Nam June Paik, TV Bra for Living Sculpture, 1969:
    • Performance with Charlotte Moorman: nude body, cello and TV sets.
    • “Captures art in the 4th dimension – TIME”.
    • Part of Fluxus – experimental, cross‑media movement of 1960s–70s.
  • Global Groove, 1973:
    • Colour videotape; scripts manipulated by video synthesizer.
    • Early exploration of video art and media overload.
  • Computer Imaging:
    • Work like Nora, 1979: computer‑generated colour photography.
    • Since 1970s, computer becomes an artistic medium; raises question whether machines will replace human artists.

Projection Mapping & Multimedia Installations

  • Tony Oursler:
    • Mansheshe, 1997; Caricature, 2002:
      • Ceramic, glass, video players, projectors, sound.
      • Digitally processed videos projected onto sculptural forms to make them feel “alive”.
      • Early example of projection mapping.
  • Key terms:
    • Multimedia presentation – enlarged projection and interactive experiences using text, audio, video, animation.
    • Projection mapping – projection on irregular 3D surfaces to create illusion of moving objects.
    • Augmented Reality (AR) & Virtual Reality (VR).
    • LED installations.

Museum as “Experience Architecture”

  • Museum no longer just a display space; becomes designed experience.
  • Whole visit choreographed to enhance interaction between visitor and environment.
  • Goal: immersive experience – engagement of all senses, temporary illusion of transcendence.

Light & Space: James Turrell

  • Afrum, Pale Blue, 1968:
    • Projected light forms illusion of hovering cube.
    • Light itself becomes “material”.
  • Works explore:
    • Perception and materiality of light.
    • Sensory experience of space, colour, and seeing.
  • Ganzfelds (Dhatu, Open Field):
    • Visitor experiences loss of depth perception; “sees herself seeing”.
    • Presents transcendental experience via pure light and minimal environment.
    • Often site‑specific (e.g. Chichu Art Museum by Tadao Ando).

Yayoi Kusama & Infinity Rooms

  • Aftermath of Obliteration of Eternity, 2009:
    • Mirrors + LED lights; creates endless reflections.
  • Infinity Mirror Room – Phalli’s Field, 1965:
    • Room filled with soft phallic forms and mirrors.
    • Combines repetition, sexuality, and infinite visual field.

teamLab & Post‑Digital Immersion

  • teamLab founded 2001:
    • Interdisciplinary group: artists, programmers, engineers, CG animators, architects, designers, etc.
    • Merges visual art, technology, design, and nature at unprecedented scale.
    • Works are interactive and reactive – change with viewer movement.
  • teamLab Borderless (2018) & teamLab Planets (2018, Tokyo):
    • Immersive worlds like:
      • (Infinite) Crystal Universe.
      • Multi Jumping Universe.
      • Universe of Water Particles on a Rock Hill where People Gather.
    • Blurs line between museum and theme park.

Art, Entertainment & Popular Culture

  • Immersive art influences and is influenced by film and theme parks:
    • Avatar’s Tree of Souls; Pandora – The World of Avatar at Disneyland (2017).
    • Life of Pi (2012) with digital oceans and animals.
  • Blurring boundary between high art and mass culture.
  • Globalization: expanded art market draws worldwide attention, but does it reflect true artistic value?
  • Digital tech and AI constantly challenge:
    • Definition of artwork and artist.
    • Role of virtual vs real experience – can online/VR replace physical contact with art?

21st‑Century Artist, Audience & Technology

  • Past: artists seen as craftsmen or intellectuals; audience expected to seek meaning and value.
  • 21st century:
    • Artist can be technocrat, anybody, even AI.
    • Audience often seeking immediate, sensual, emotional experience.
  • Rise of immersive art:
    • Works grow in size and complexity.
    • Interact with audience in new ways; experiences are transient.
    • Question: are museums becoming fun parks?
  • Technologies become both tool and content:
    • But can technology replace content (idea, meaning)?
    • What impact will digital tech and AI bring to the future of art?
