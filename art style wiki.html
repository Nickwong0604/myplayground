Western Art Mini‑Wiki

Single‑file mini‑reference for your Western Art Appreciation finals. All topics from Classical Art through Modern Art are organized in lecture order and written in exam‑oriented language.

How to Use This File Exam‑oriented

  • Use the sidebar to jump to periods & themes in the same order as your lecture notes.
  • Each page highlights:
    • Key ideas & terminology (good for MC / T‑F / fill‑in‑the‑blank / short‑answer).
    • Core artworks & examples with cause‑and‑effect links (good for essays).
  • Check Comparison Tables for quick style differences.
  • See Exam Strategy for Section A & Section B tips.

Western Art Appreciation

Western Art Appreciation is not just about memorizing artworks. For your exam, you are expected to:

  • Recognize styles and periods from visual features and key terms.
  • Connect art with context (religion, politics, technology, philosophy).
  • Use precise terminology (e.g. contrapposto, tenebrism, readymade).
  • Compare movements in terms of ideas (e.g. humanism vs Counter‑Reformation rhetoric) and style (line vs colour, calm vs drama).

Section A of the exam tests recognition and terminology. Section B requires short essays that:

  • Answer a “discuss / explain / compare” question clearly.
  • Use named artworks and link them to ideas, techniques and historical context.

Classical Art 1: Greek Periods & Canon

Chronological Framework

  • Archaic period (9th c. BC – 480 BC)
    • Birth of independent city‑states (poleis).
    • First large‑scale stone sculpture; experimentation with life‑size human figures.
  • Classical period (c. 480 – 323 BC)
    • 479 BC: Pericles defeats Persians → Athens enters its Golden Age.
    • Art reaches a “zenith” with humanism, rationality, and the canon of proportions.
  • Hellenistic period (c. 323 – 146/30 BC)
    • 323 BC: Alexander the Great dies, his empire fragmented.
    • 146 BC: Greece under Roman rule; 30 BC: Ptolemaic Egypt falls to Rome.

Key Ideas

Humanism

Classical Greek art places the human being at the centre of the universe. The ideal human body represents both physical strength and rational mind. This “perfect” human is almost divine.

Symmetry, Harmony & Proportion

  • Greek artists believe beauty is based on mathematical proportion.
  • Pythagorean ratios (2:1, 3:2, 4:3) and later theories (Vitruvius) shape both art and architecture.
  • The human body becomes a model: e.g. head : total body = 1 : 8 in Polykleitos’ canon.

The Vitruvian Man

Leonardo’s drawing “Vitruvian Man” (c. 1490) visualizes the ancient Roman architect Vitruvius’ ideas:

  • Human body fits perfectly into a circle and a square.
  • Every part has a proportional relation to the whole (face : body = 1:10; head : body = 1:8; torso : body = 1:4).
  • This shows a Renaissance belief: nature is rationally ordered, so art should imitate that order.

Archaic Kouroi & Early Greek Sculpture

Egyptian Background

Example: King Menkaure and Queen, Old Kingdom Egypt (2490–2472 BC).

  • Compact, frontal, rigid pose.
  • Aim: convey eternity and immortality, not individuality.
  • Formal and standardized for centuries.

Emergence of Kouroi

Early Greek sculptors adapt Egyptian formulas but innovate:

  • Kouros, c. 590–580 BC:
    • Life‑size, freestanding statue of a nude youth.
    • Frontal, symmetrical, one leg advanced (Egyptian influence).
    • Nudity highlights physical perfection of young athletes.
  • Later kouroi (e.g. Anavysos/Kroisos Kouros) show:
    • More natural proportions, softer modeling of muscles.
    • The “Archaic smile” – suggests life and movement.
    • Used as votive offerings or grave markers, celebrating eternal youthful beauty.

From Egyptian Eternity to Greek Lifelikeness

The main shift from Egyptian to Archaic Greek sculpture is a move from timeless, rigid forms to figures that attempt to look alive and individual, even while keeping a formal, frontal pose.

Classical Canon & Contrapposto

Contrapposto

Contrapposto is an asymmetrical balance of the body:

  • Weight rests on one leg (the “engaged” leg), while the other leg is relaxed.
  • Hips and shoulders tilt in opposite directions.
  • Creates a sense of potential movement and natural posture.

Kritios Boy (c. 480 BC) is the first known statue fully composed in contrapposto:

  • Archaic smile disappears; expression is calm and serious.
  • Body looks more anatomically accurate and lifelike.

Polykleitos and the Canon

Sculptor Polykleitos formulates a mathematical Canon of ideal male proportions in his statue Doryphoros (Spear Bearer) (450–440 BC):

  • Head : total body ≈ 1 : 8.
  • Well‑defined musculature, balanced contrapposto.
  • Embodies Greek ideal: physical beauty (athletic body) + goodness of soul (rational control) = perfection.

Perfection is considered a divine quality, so the ideal male nude links human and divine.

Harmony & Symmetry

Classical art values:

  • Symmetry: balance of parts, including contrapposto as dynamic symmetry.
  • Harmony: pleasing proportion between parts and whole, often using numerical ratios.

Hellenistic Art & Pathos

Shift from Ideal Calm to Drama & Emotion

In the Hellenistic period (after Alexander the Great), artists:

  • Violate or exaggerate the Classical canon.
  • Explore exotic subjects (barbarians, satyrs, drunkards).
  • Emphasize drama, emotion, and eroticism over calm ideal beauty.

Humanized & Moving Figures

  • Hermes Carrying the Infant Dionysos (attributed to Praxiteles):
    • S‑curve adds sensuality and grace.
    • Gentle interaction between god and baby humanizes the divine.
  • Apoxyomenos (Scraper) (Lysippos):
    • Head : body ≈ 1 : 9 → taller, slenderer body.
    • Arms extended into viewer’s space, body twisting → increased motion.

Pathos and the “Noble Foe”

  • Dying Gaul:
    • Shows a barbarian (Gaul) with moustache and neck ring.
    • Portrayed as brave and noble in defeat.
    • Face and posture express intense pain and suffering (pathos).
    • Later influences Michelangelo and Baroque sculptors.
  • Ludovisi Gaul:
    • Gallic warrior kills himself and his wife rather than be enslaved.
    • Conveys despair, heroism, and dramatic movement.

Erotic Sensuality

Sleeping Satyr (Barberini Faun):

  • Depicts a drunken, sleeping satyr with exposed, twisting body.
  • Classical ideal of rational control is replaced by unconsciousness and sexuality.
  • Nudity is used for erotic desire, anticipating Baroque sensuality.

Roman Art & Portraiture

General Features of Roman Art

  • Adopts Greek idealism but adds pragmatism and functionalism.
  • Oscillates between realism (verism) and idealism.
  • Serves the imperial cult: glorification of emperors and empire.
  • Spreads Classical forms throughout Roman territories → model for later European rulers.

Julius Caesar: Real Portraiture & Propaganda

  • Denarius with portrait of Julius Caesar (44 BC):
    • First Roman to put his own likeness on coinage.
    • Shows ageing face, lined forehead, realistic features.
    • Purpose: political propaganda – promote his achievements across the empire.
  • Tusculum Portrait:
    • Provides likeness of Caesar shortly before his death.
    • Emphasizes physical imperfections (bulging skull, sparse hair).
    • He is shown as a man, not yet deified.

Augustus: Return to Idealism

  • Augustus Bevilacqua:
    • Adopts Greek practice of depicting rulers as ageless and idealized.
    • Face is serene, elegant, somewhat aloof.
  • Augustus of Primaporta:
    • Full‑length idealized statue; barefoot like a hero or god.
    • Breastplate reliefs emphasize him as military victor and bringer of peace (Pax Romana).
    • State portraits like this were copied and displayed throughout the empire.

Hadrian & Later Emperors

  • Portrait Bust of Hadrian:
    • First Roman emperor to wear a beard, signalling admiration for Greek culture.
    • Modeled after the portrait of Pericles by Kresilas; sets a fashion for later emperors.
  • Equestrian Statue of Marcus Aurelius:
    • Bronze equestrian monuments honouring emperors were common civic symbols.
    • The emperor dominates the horse, yet his face appears thoughtful and weary – a philosopher‑king.
  • Portrait of Caracalla:
    • Late imperial portrait with intense, almost paranoid expression.
    • Shows anxiety and aggressiveness, mirroring the instability of the period.

Reliefs, Altars, Arches & Columns

Ara Pacis Augustae (Altar of Augustan Peace)

Built to celebrate peace restored by Augustus. Relief friezes show:

  • Procession of imperial family and officials.
  • Recognizable individuals (e.g. Agrippa, Livia or Julia, Augustus) combined with ideal figures.
  • Inspired by the Athenian Panathenaic Procession on the Parthenon frieze, but with more realism and historical specificity.

Arch of Titus

  • Triumphal arch built in Rome (AD 81) to celebrate Emperor Titus’ victory in the Jewish War.
  • Relief of soldiers carrying spoils from the Temple of Jerusalem (e.g., the golden menorah).
  • Figures carved at varying depths create a sense of movement and three‑dimensional space.

Trajan’s Column

  • Spiral relief tells the story of Dacian Wars in about 150 episodes.
  • Combines narrative history with repetitive images of the emperor as military leader.

Roman Wall Painting: The Four Styles

First Style – Masonry / Incrustation Style

  • Walls covered with plaster and stucco to imitate coloured marble slabs.
  • Focus on flat surface; decorative, but no depth illusion.
  • Example: Samnite House, Herculaneum (late 2nd c. BC).

Second Style – Architectural / Illusionistic Style

  • Painted architecture creates illusion of extended space behind the wall.
  • Views of colonnades, gardens, landscapes, and figures seen through fake windows.
  • Looks rational and naturalistic, a Roman invention of pictorial depth.
  • Example: Villa of the Mysteries (Mysteries of Dionysus Ritual) – stage‑like architecture framing a frieze of life‑size figures.

Third Style – Ornate Style

  • Reacting against deep illusion; wall is emphasized as flat again.
  • Uniform bright colour background; delicate, fanciful architectural motifs (“jeweller’s architecture”).
  • Small pictures or figures “float” in the centre, with little attempt at spatial depth.
  • Example: House of the Caccia Antica, Pompeii.

Fourth Style – Composite Style

  • Emerges after earthquake of AD 62; combines elements from Second and Third Styles.
  • Rational architectural frames suggesting depth alternate with floating pictures and decorative panels.
  • Example: House of the Vettii, Pompeii – fake architecture plus framed mythological panels.

Roman Painting Themes & Legacy

Common Themes

  • History paintings – e.g. Brawl in the Pompeii Amphitheater records a real riot during a gladiatorial show.
  • Mythological scenes – e.g. Polyphemus and Galatea, set in idyllic landscapes.
  • Portraits – e.g. Portraits of Terentius Neo and his Wife, life‑like and used in election propaganda.
  • Genre scenes – everyday life such as Sale of Bread.
  • Idyllic / pastoral landscapes – peaceful rural scenes.
  • Still lifes – fruit, bread, wine; careful observation of surfaces.

Impact on Later Art

Roman illusionistic painting strongly influences:

  • Renaissance perspective and architectural backgrounds.
  • Baroque stage‑like compositions and dramatic interior decoration.
  • Interest in genre scenes and still life in later European painting traditions.

Late Antique & Early Christian Art

If this content is not heavily emphasized in your course, you can keep this as a light reference. Late Antique art marks a transition from Classical naturalism to the more symbolic style of Christian art.

Byzantine Art

Byzantine art favours frontal, iconic images, gold backgrounds, and spiritual symbolism. It continues and transforms Roman imperial imagery into Christian imperial imagery.

Islamic Art of the Mediterranean

Islamic art of this region often emphasizes geometric pattern, calligraphy, and vegetal motifs; figural representation is limited in religious contexts but used in palatial and secular art.

Renaissance in Italy · Overview

What “Renaissance” Means

  • Renaissance = “rebirth” of Classical (Greek and Roman) forms and values.
  • Key concepts:
    • Classicism – Classical art as inspiration and model.
    • Humanism – man‑centered culture; focus on the individual and worldly experience.
    • Rationalism – scientific, logical approach to nature and space.

Materials & Techniques

  • Oil paint replaces tempera:
    • Allows smooth tonal modeling and rich chiaroscuro.
    • Dries slowly, so artists can rework surfaces.
  • Canvas replaces wood panels in many regions:
    • Lighter and more flexible – good for large works.
  • Linear perspective:
    • Mathematical system to create illusion of depth.
    • All orthogonals converge at a vanishing point on the horizon.
  • Compositions use geometric forms (pyramids, circles, squares) and careful proportions.

Early Renaissance: Masaccio & Donatello

Masaccio (Painting)

The Virgin and Child (1426)

  • Pyramidal composition stabilizes the group.
  • Linear perspective used in the throne; foreshortened halo of Christ.
  • Figures are solid, weighty; cast shadows indicate real human presence.

Tribute Money (Brancacci Chapel, 1424–25)

  • Uses continuous narrative: three episodes with Peter in one picture.
  • Figures have monumental, sculptural character influenced by Roman sculpture.
  • Linear perspective organizes rational architecture and landscape.

Trinity (Santa Maria Novella, 1426–28)

  • Perfect illusion of a deep architectural niche painted on a flat wall.
  • One‑point perspective creates logical space from the viewer’s eye level (looking up into the vault).
  • Provides a visual demonstration of Renaissance rationalism and religious hierarchy.

Donatello (Sculpture)

David (c. 1430–40)

  • First free‑standing male nude statue since antiquity.
  • Biblical hero David shown as a graceful youth after defeating Goliath.
  • Pose and proportions recall Greek nude gods; relaxed contrapposto.

Gattamelata (Erasmo da Narni, c. 1445–53)

  • Bronze equestrian statue in Padua.
  • Revives Roman equestrian monument tradition.
  • Humanist aim: celebrate individual achievement and character.

High Renaissance: Leonardo, Michelangelo, Raphael

Leonardo da Vinci

Virgin of the Rocks (1483–85)

  • Pyramidal grouping of Virgin, Child and angels.
  • Aerial perspective: landscape fades into bluish haze.
  • No architectural frame; figures integrated with nature.

Last Supper (1495–97)

  • Experimental fresco technique with oil and tempera (unfortunately unstable).
  • One‑point perspective; vanishing point located behind Christ’s head (centre of composition and emotion).
  • 12 apostles arranged in 4 groups of 3, reacting to Christ’s announcement of betrayal.

Mona Lisa (1503–06)

  • Uses sfumato (smoky transitions) around face and hands.
  • Aerial perspective unites sitter with mysterious landscape.
  • Focus on psychological depth and subtle expression.

Michelangelo

Story of Genesis (Sistine Chapel Ceiling, 1508–12)

  • Ceiling conceived as illusory architecture with painted pilasters, prophets, sibyls, ancestors of Christ.
  • Central 9 panels narrate Creation, Fall, and story of Noah.

Creation of Adam

  • God reaches out to give life to Adam, who is a classical ideal youth.
  • Humanism: man is created in God’s image; human body is glorified.

David (1501–04)

  • Colossal marble statue (over 5 m high).
  • Depicts David before battle; tension and concentration within an ideal, balanced body.
  • Embodies harmony between reason and physical power.

Raphael

Madonna of the Meadow (1506) & Alba Madonna (c. 1510)

  • Madonna and Child with young John the Baptist.
  • Calm, elegant figures, subtle chiaroscuro (influenced by Leonardo) and solid forms (influenced by Michelangelo).

School of Athens (1509)

  • Fresco in the Pope’s library (Stanza della Segnatura) representing Philosophy.
  • Ancient philosophers (e.g. Plato, Aristotle) depicted as statuesque figures; some with portraits of contemporary artists.
  • Perfect linear perspective and monumental architecture create a harmonious “humanist world”.

Venetian Renaissance & Colourism

Materials & Environment

  • Venice adopts oil paint and canvas earlier, partly due to humid climate unsuited to fresco.
  • Venetian artists develop colourism: building form and texture primarily with colour.

Titian

Assumption (1516–18)

  • Large altarpiece with dynamic composition divided into a circle and a square.
  • Rich golden tones, dominated by red and yellow.
  • Combines rational structure with drama and movement.

Venus before the Mirror (1555)

  • Warm, glowing flesh achieved through multiple glazes of oil paint.
  • Glossy gold and intense red emphasizing sensuality.

Veronese

Banquet in the House of Levi (1573)

  • Originally titled Last Supper; authorities forced the name change because of its worldly pageantry.
  • Vast composition, elaborate architecture, rich costumes, and silvery tones.

Baroque Art in Italy · Caravaggio & Bernini

Historical Context & Style

  • Driven by the Counter‑Reformation of the Catholic Church.
  • Artists aim to move and persuade viewers emotionally, using:
    • Realism combined with theatricality.
    • Strong contrasts of light and dark.
    • Dynamic compositions with diagonals.
    • Rich colours and intense narratives.

Caravaggio: Realism & Tenebrism

  • Transforms sacred and mythological themes into scenes with everyday people, often unidealized and rough.
  • Composition often compressed into the foreground, confronting the viewer with “ugly truth”.
  • Uses tenebrism (“dark manner”): deep shadows with spotlight‑like illumination to focus narrative.

Key Works

  • Still Life with Basket of Fruit:
    • Meticulous realism (Flemish influence); rotting fruit symbolizes transient beauty and life’s brevity.
  • Boy Bitten by a Lizard:
    • Dramatic expression at moment of pain; sharp light‑dark contrast.
    • Reflections in glass and minute detail echo Flemish painting.
  • Judith Beheading Holofernes:
    • Brutal beheading staged like theatre; figures emerge from dark background.
    • Tenebrism heightens drama and psychological tension.
  • Contarelli Chapel – The Calling and Martyrdom of St. Matthew:
    • The Calling of St. Matthew: diagonal beam of sacred light singles out Matthew among tax collectors in modern dress.
    • The Martyrdom of St. Matthew: stage‑like composition with Michelangelesque nudes and sacred light from above.
  • Death of the Virgin:
    • Shows Virgin as a dead, lowly woman (possibly based on a drowned prostitute) instead of idealized assumption.
    • Rejected by patrons as inappropriate – shows extremity of Caravaggio’s realism.

Caravaggisti: Artemisia Gentileschi

Her Judith Beheading Holofernes (Uffizi, c. 1620) follows Caravaggio’s tenebrism:

  • Heroic female subject; favourite theme.
  • Tight composition focuses all forces on Holofernes’s head; dynamic balance of force and counterforce.

Bernini: Sculptural Theatre

Gian Lorenzo Bernini extends Michelangelo’s legacy by:

  • Instilling motion and emotion into sculpture.
  • Designing integrated ensembles of sculpture, architecture, and light.

Sculpture Examples

  • Rape of Proserpina:
    • Narrative at the climax of struggle; Pluto’s fingers press into Proserpina’s flesh.
    • Baroque aim: emotional arousal through sensual realism.
  • David (1623–24):
    • Shows David at the moment of hurling the stone, body twisted, face intensely focused.
    • In contrast:
      DonatelloMichelangeloBernini
      Early Renaissance, calm youth after victory. High Renaissance, calm concentration before battle. Baroque, dynamic action during battle.

Illusionistic Ceilings & Jesuit Art

Counter‑Reformation & Church Interiors

  • Ceiling frescoes turn church vaults into visions of Heaven.
  • Art becomes a powerful tool to glorify and consolidate Catholic Church authority.
  • Scale and height induce awe and emotional submission.

Triumph of the Name of Jesus (Il Gesù, Rome)

  • Fresco combined with stucco figures, difficult to distinguish painting from sculpture.
  • Central monogram IHS (Name of Jesus) bursts in light, surrounded by tumbling figures.
  • Painted figures are shaded to look solid, intensifying illusion.

Glory of St. Ignatius of Loyola (Sant’Ignazio, Rome)

  • Illusion that the roof of the church is opened; Heaven continues the church space.
  • Four corners represent four continents – Europe as ruler; Jesuits sending missionaries worldwide.
  • St. Ignatius received by Christ, celebrating Jesuit contribution to triumph of Catholicism.

Cornaro Chapel & Cathedra Petri

  • Cathedra Petri (Throne of St Peter, St Peter’s Basilica):
    • Old wooden chair encased in a cloud of golden glory with angels and rays of light.
    • Typical Baroque vision of Heaven: dazzling light and joyous angels.
  • Cornaro Chapel (Santa Maria della Vittoria):
    • Bernini turns the chapel into a theatre of the mystic Ecstasy of St Teresa.
    • Hidden window above provides real light on golden rays behind the figures.
    • Members of the Cornaro family appear in sculpted balconies, witnessing the miracle and discussing it, increasing credibility.

Neoclassicism & Enlightenment

Background

  • Enlightenment: reason and knowledge should guide life.
  • Reaction against perceived insincerity and extravagance of Baroque and Rococo.
  • Excavations of Pompeii and Herculaneum revive interest in Classical austerity and virtue.

Jacques‑Louis David

Oath of the Horatii (1784)

  • Roman legend of three Horatii brothers swearing to defend Rome.
  • Doric architecture in background (masculinity, severity).
  • Clear, symmetrical composition; statuesque male figures vs emotional women.
  • Message: patriotism and sacrifice for the state.

Lictors Returning to Brutus the Bodies of His Sons (1789)

  • Brutus orders execution of his sons for treason against the Republic.
  • Brutus sits in shadow, grieving in silence; women in light, display intense emotion.
  • Again highlights civic virtue and tensions between public duty and private feeling.

Death of Marat (1793)

  • Marat, a radical revolutionary, murdered in his bath.
  • Simple, austere setting; strong light on Marat’s idealized, calm body.
  • Combines history with portraiture to turn a controversial figure into a “martyr” of the Revolution.

Intervention of the Sabine Women (1799)

  • Sabine women stopping battle between their Roman husbands and Sabine fathers.
  • Heroic nude figures in classical style; theme of reconciliation after political conflict.

Ingres

  • Student of David; admires Raphael and Florentine line.
  • Advocates linearism (drawing) over colour.

Mademoiselle Caroline Riviere (1806)

  • Simple pose, pale palette, strong linear outline.
  • Conveys purity and youthful femininity; minimal decoration.

Madame Moitessier (1856)

  • Highly finished, decorative portrait.
  • Flattened forms, elongated limbs; stylized contours and anatomy typical of Ingres.

Romanticism & the Sublime

Key Ideas

  • Opposes Neoclassical emphasis on cold reason and order.
  • Values imagination, intuition, and strong feelings.
  • Search for freedom through subjective emotion and fantasy.
  • Fascination with:
    • The past and the exotic (Orientalism).
    • Nature as powerful and sometimes terrifying.
    • Dreams, madness, human vices.
  • Sublime: aesthetic of awe, terror, and danger that excites and overwhelms.

Goya

Sleep of Reason Produces Monsters (1799)

  • Etching from Los Caprichos.
  • Artist sleeping at desk; bats and owls swarm around.
  • Inscription: when reason sleeps, imagination produces monsters; united with reason, it creates marvels.

Second & Third of May, 1808 (painted 1814)

  • Uprising of the Second of May: chaotic street fight in Madrid against French troops.
  • Executions of the Third of May: anonymous Spanish rebels executed by firing squad.
  • Central man in white shirt resembles Christ, arms outstretched – a modern martyr.
  • French soldiers are shown as faceless machine, emphasizing brutality.

Severed Limbs (1818)

  • Oil sketch of dismembered limbs, related to a murder/cannibalism case.
  • Combines horror with analytical observation, creating “perverse beauty”.

Géricault & Delacroix

Géricault, Raft of the Medusa (1818–19)

  • Depicts aftermath of real shipwreck; 150 people left on a raft, only 10 survived.
  • Reports of cannibalism fascinated the artist.
  • Diagonal composition, dramatic light, and twisting bodies emphasize horror and struggle for survival.

Delacroix, Death of Sardanapalus (1827)

  • Inspired by Byron’s poem about Assyrian king who orders destruction of palace and killing of his entourage.
  • Exotic Oriental setting; swirling composition of violence and sensuality.

Liberty Leading the People (1830)

  • Commemorates July Revolution in France.
  • Liberty personified as a woman carrying the tricolour flag, leading people over barricades.
  • Blend of allegory (myth) and modern history; Romantic colour and energy.

Friedrich & Turner

Caspar David Friedrich

  • Monk by the Sea: tiny figure facing a vast expanse of sea and sky – nature as manifestation of God, but also overwhelming.
  • Abbey in the Oak Forest: funeral procession among ruins; gloomy, nihilistic view that religion does not guarantee salvation.

J. M. W. Turner, Slave Ship (1840)

  • Based on case where English traders threw sick slaves overboard to collect insurance.
  • Stormy sea and fiery sunset dominate; drowning bodies are barely visible.
  • Nature’s terrifying power and human cruelty combined in one sublime image.

Impressionism & Neo‑Impressionism

Technical Background

  • Industrial paint tubes (from 1840s) make oil painting portable and cheap.
  • Artists can work en plein air (in open air) and finish works outdoors.
  • Industrial printing and colour studies spread colour theory, including complementary colours.

Impressionist Style

  • Vibrant colour palette; systematic use of complementary colours and coloured shadows.
  • Colours laid side by side (not blended smoothly); human eye mixes them optically.
  • Rapid brushwork, impasto, broken strokes, sketchy finish.
  • Blurry edges and ill‑defined forms → anti‑academic finish.

Redefining Realism

  • Realism becomes optical, not detailed: capturing fleeting effects of light and atmosphere.
  • Paintings finished outdoors under specific weather conditions.
  • Subjective viewpoint but relatively neutral emotion; emphasis on “here and now” of modern life.

Monet

  • Impression: Sunrise (1872): hazy view of harbour; critic’s insult “Impressionists” becomes group name.
  • Women in the Garden (1866): painted entirely outdoors; form defined by bright and dark tones.
  • La Grenouillère (1869, with Renoir): coarse, broken brushstrokes; sunlight and water movement.
  • Gare Saint‑Lazare (1877): steam, iron, and glass; atmospheric study of modern industry.
  • Poppy Field, Autumn Effect at Argenteuil: refined use of colour, exclusion of black from palette.
  • Waterlilies series (over 250 paintings, including huge panels at Orangerie):
    • Use of cobalt violet and other industrial pigments.
    • Late works verge on abstraction; focus on colour and surface of water.

Manet, Renoir, Degas

  • Manet:
    • Music in the Tuileries Gardens: modern Parisian crowd enjoying music.
    • Waitress with Beer Glasses: café as centre of social life.
  • Renoir:
    • Ball at the Moulin de la Galette: working‑class dance in open air; interplay of sunlight and gaslight.
  • Degas:
    • Dancer with Bouquet, Saluting on Stage: effects of low‑angle stage lighting; coloured shadows.
    • Dancer Looking at the Sole of her Right Foot (sculpture): rough texture, fleeting pose, movement captured.

Neo‑Impressionism (Seurat)

  • Critic Fénéon coins term in 1886.
  • Scientific approach to colour and composition; reaction to “sloppiness” of Impressionism.
  • Uses regular dots or strokes (pointillism), clear contours, and carefully balanced compositions.

A Sunday on La Grande Jatte (1884–86):

  • Studio work over two years; no on‑site improvisation.
  • Scene of modern leisure but organized like a frieze; timeless and serene.

Post‑Impressionism

General

Term coined by critic Roger Fry in 1910. Artists build upon Impressionist colour and brushwork but recover expressive structure and meaning through form and colour.

Cézanne

Montagne Sainte‑Victoire with Large Pine (1885–87)

  • Simplifies fields into green rectangles, mountains into blue segments.
  • Uses contour lines and patches of colour to suggest volume and solidity.
  • Bridges Impressionism and Cubism by reducing nature to geometric forms.

Card Players (c. 1890–95)

  • Figures and objects reduced to cylinders, spheres, trapezoids.
  • Creates a new sense of clarity and weight in everyday scenes.

Gauguin

Vision After the Sermon (1888)

  • Breton women see a religious vision after a sermon.
  • Flat, opaque fields of pure colour (dominant red) separated by dark outlines.
  • Colour itself carries spiritual and emotional meaning.

Van Gogh

Self‑Portrait with Portrait of Émile Bernard (Les Misérables)

  • Shows himself and friend Bernard as poor, struggling artists.
  • Uses strong complementary contrasts (yellow vs blue‑green) to express emotion.

Night Café (1888)

  • Interior of a café in Arles, with strange red and green colours.
  • Van Gogh writes that he wants to express “terrible passions” of humanity.
  • Systematic brushstrokes around glowing lamps; perspective and colour create a claustrophobic, hell‑like atmosphere.

Late Works

  • Self‑Portraits with bandaged ear and pipe: show recovery after mental crisis; use strong colour contrasts and broad strokes.
  • Wheatfield with Crows (1890): dark, turbulent sky, black crows, and divided path suggest inner turmoil and possible contemplation of suicide.

Modern Art: Cubism to Pop Art

General Shift

  • 20th century sees a break with traditional academic art.
  • Photography takes over faithful reproduction of reality.
  • Artists abandon illusionism and naturalism, exploring abstraction.
  • Art is redefined; value placed on creativity, originality, and experiment.

Cubism (Picasso, Braque)

  • Develops in stages: Proto‑Cubism, Analytical Cubism, Synthetic Cubism.
  • Rejects single‑point perspective and traditional modeling.
  • Fragments forms into small planes and recombines them to show multiple viewpoints simultaneously.
  • Evokes the “fourth dimension” (time) through shifting planes.

Key Works

  • Picasso, Les Demoiselles d’Avignon (1907):
    • Brothel scene; figures inspired by Iberian sculpture, Egyptian art, and African masks.
    • Space and bodies fractured into sharp, angular forms.
  • Picasso, Portrait of Daniel‑Henry Kahnweiler (1910):
    • Analytical Cubism; almost monochrome.
    • Forms analyzed into facets around multiple axes.
  • Picasso, Still Life with Chair Caning (1912):
    • Synthetic Cubism; uses collage (oilcloth printed like chair caning) and a real rope frame.
    • Real objects used to create a painting; painting used to imitate real objects → blurs boundary.

German Expressionism

  • Expresses inner emotions and spiritual concerns using distortion and intense colour.
  • Reacts to social tension, poverty, and looming war.

Examples

  • Käthe Kollwitz, Woman with Dead Child (1903):
    • Engraving and etching; bodies drastically deformed to express grief.
    • Monochrome, sombre mood.
  • Ernst Ludwig Kirchner, Berlin Street Scene (1913):
    • Figures distorted into jagged forms; garish colours.
    • Reflects decadence and tension in pre‑WWI Berlin.
  • Kandinsky, Composition VI (1913):
    • Abstract painting; uses colour and line like music to convey spiritual meaning.

Futurism (Italy, Boccioni)

  • Italy wants to break from burden of Classical past.
  • Celebrates revolution, war, speed, machines, and modern cities.
  • Sees violence as a way to destroy old culture and create the new.
  • States of Mind I: The Farewells (1911):
    • Railway station; soldiers leaving for war.
    • Interlocking lines and planes create a sense of motion and tension; influenced by Cubism.
  • Unique Forms of Continuity in Space (1913):
    • Striding figure merging with surrounding space.
    • Mechanical, aerodynamic body; symbolizes speed and modernization.

De Stijl & Neo‑Plasticism (Mondrian, Rietveld)

  • Dutch movement; artists publish the magazine De Stijl (“The Style”).
  • Neo‑Plasticism aims to express universal harmony through:
    • Primary colours (red, yellow, blue) and neutrals (white, grey, black).
    • Vertical and horizontal black lines only.
  • Mondrian, Composition with Red, Black, Blue, Yellow and Grey (1920):
    • Balanced grid of coloured rectangles; expresses mystical unity of the universe as balanced tensions.
  • Composition with Yellow, Blue and Red (1937–42):
    • Red appears to advance, blue to recede, yellow to expand; spatial effect achieved purely by colour.
  • Rietveld, Red and Blue Chair (1918):
    • Applies De Stijl principles to furniture design (industrial application).

Dada (Zurich, New York, Paris)

  • Emerges during WWI; artists blame Western faith in rational progress for leading to war.
  • Rejects reason; embraces chance, absurdity, and provocation.
  • Jean Arp, Collage Arranged According to the Laws of Chance:
    • Torn paper squares dropped randomly and glued where they land.
    • Introduces automatism (automatic process) as creative method.
  • Marcel Duchamp, Fountain (1917):
    • Factory‑made urinal signed “R. Mutt, 1917”.
    • Example of a readymade: ordinary object presented as art.
    • Challenges authority and poses question: Who defines art?

Surrealism

  • Influenced by Freud’s psychoanalysis and Dada’s irrationality.
  • Aims to reveal deeper reality of dreams and unconscious.
  • Uses bizarre juxtapositions and symbolic imagery.
  • Dalí, Persistence of Memory (1931):
    • Desert landscape with melting clocks; familiar objects rendered with miniature precision.
    • Presented in unnatural situations to create dream‑like absurdity.
  • Picasso, Girl Before a Mirror (1932):
    • Mirror image represents alter ego; colourful, fragmented forms.
    • Explores psychological complexity of identity.

Pop Art

  • Starts in 1950s (Britain) and 1960s (USA).
  • Responds to consumer culture, mass media, advertising.
  • Uses easily recognizable imagery, bright colours, flat style and repetition.
  • Roy Lichtenstein, Whaam! (1963):
    • Blown‑up comic book panel of fighter plane battle.
    • Uses hand‑painted Ben‑Day dots (via metal mesh) to mimic industrial printing.
  • Andy Warhol, Campbell’s Soup Cans (1962):
    • 32 canvases of soup cans arranged like supermarket shelves or factory line.
  • Green Marilyn & Mao:
    • Silkscreen portraits of celebrities and political leaders.
    • Flat, garish colours; emphasize how media turns people into reproducible icons.

Glossary · Key Terms

  • Archaic smile – slight smile on Archaic Greek statues suggesting life.
  • Contrapposto – asymmetrical pose where weight is on one leg, creating natural balance and movement.
  • Canon (of Polykleitos) – system of ideal body proportions (head:body ≈ 1:8) for the perfect male nude.
  • Pathos – expression of strong emotion and suffering, especially in Hellenistic sculpture.
  • Verism – extreme realism, often emphasizing age and flaws (Roman portraits).
  • En plein air – painting outdoors from direct observation.
  • Chiaroscuro – modeling form with strong contrasts of light and dark.
  • Tenebrism – extreme chiaroscuro with dark backgrounds and strong spotlighting (Caravaggio).
  • Sfumato – smoky, blurred transitions between light and shadow (Leonardo).
  • Linear perspective – mathematical system to represent three‑dimensional space on a flat surface.
  • Aerial perspective – effect of atmosphere making distant objects lighter and bluer.
  • Illusionism – creating convincing impressions of real space or objects.
  • Readymade – ordinary manufactured object designated as art (Duchamp).
  • Collage – artwork made by assembling paper, photographs or other materials on a surface.
  • Pointillism – Neo‑Impressionist technique of small colour dots that blend optically.
  • Sublime – aesthetic category involving awe, terror and vastness (Romanticism).
  • Neo‑Plasticism – Mondrian’s style using only primary colours and straight lines to express universal harmony.

Materials & Techniques

  • Fresco – painting on wet plaster; common in Renaissance and Baroque ceilings.
  • Tempera – pigment mixed with egg yolk; used in medieval and early Renaissance panel painting.
  • Oil paint – pigment in oil binder; slow‑drying, allows blending and glazing.
  • Canvas – fabric support, lighter than wooden panel; favoured in Venice and later periods.
  • Bronze – metal used for durable sculpture (e.g. equestrian statues, Bernini works).
  • Marble – stone for sculpture and architecture (Greek, Roman, Renaissance, Baroque).
  • Engraving / Etching / Aquatint – printmaking techniques using metal plates.
  • Silkscreen (screenprint) – stencil‑based printing used by Pop artists (Warhol).
  • Collage & Assemblage – combining different materials (paper, objects) in one artwork.

Comparison Tables (Style vs Style)

Classical vs Hellenistic Greek Sculpture

Classical Hellenistic
Ideal Calm, balanced, rational; ideal youth. Drama, strong emotion, exotic subjects.
Form Canon of proportions; contrapposto. Violated canon; exaggerated poses and musculature.
Example Polykleitos’ Doryphoros. Dying Gaul, Sleeping Satyr.

Renaissance vs Baroque Religious Art

Renaissance Baroque
Goal Clarity, rational order, humanist ideals. Emotional persuasion, drama, sensory impact.
Space Stable perspective, geometric composition. Dynamic diagonals, illusionistic space, ceiling openings.
Light Even, natural light (chiaroscuro). Strong contrasts (tenebrism), hidden light sources.
Examples Masaccio Trinity, Raphael School of Athens. Caravaggio Contarelli Chapel, Bernini Cornaro Chapel.

Neoclassicism vs Romanticism

Neoclassicism Romanticism
Ideas Reason, civic virtue, order, clarity. Emotion, imagination, individual freedom, sublime.
Style Clear line, balanced composition, calm figures. Dynamic compositions, strong colour, dramatic scenes.
Examples David’s Oath of the Horatii. Goya’s Third of May, Delacroix’s Liberty.

Impressionism vs Post‑Impressionism

Impressionism Post‑Impressionism
Focus Light effects, fleeting moments, optical realism. Structure, expressive colour, personal meaning.
Brushwork Loose, broken, spontaneous, en plein air. More controlled; colour and form shaped to express ideas.
Examples Monet’s Waterlilies. Cézanne’s Montagne Sainte‑Victoire, Van Gogh’s Night Café.

Exam Strategy & Sample Prompts

Section A: MC / T‑F / Fill‑in‑the‑Blank / Short Questions

  • Memorize key terms (see Glossary) and be able to match them to:
    • Definitions (e.g. tenebrism, contrapposto, readymade).
    • Artists and periods (e.g. Caravaggio with Baroque, Mondrian with De Stijl).
  • Know cause‑and‑effect:
    • Counter‑Reformation → Baroque drama.
    • Industrial paint tubes → Impressionist en plein air.
    • WWI → Dada’s rejection of rationality.
  • Practice explaining a term in 2–3 clear sentences with an example.

Section B: Essay Writing

Typical verbs: discuss / explain / compare. Always:

  • Define the key terms and time periods in your first paragraph.
  • Give at least two artworks per side when comparing.
  • Link style to context (religion, politics, technology, philosophy).

Sample Essay Prompts

  1. Compare the aims and styles of Renaissance and Baroque religious art.
    – Use examples such as Masaccio’s Trinity and Caravaggio’s Calling of St. Matthew, or Michelangelo’s David and Bernini’s David.
  2. Discuss how Neoclassicism and Romanticism reflect different attitudes toward reason and emotion.
    – Use David’s Oath of the Horatii vs Goya’s Third of May or Delacroix’s Liberty Leading the People.
  3. Explain how Impressionism and Post‑Impressionism changed the concept of realism in art.
    – Compare Monet’s optical realism with Cézanne’s structural realism and Van Gogh/Gauguin’s expressive colour.
  4. Analyse the impact of new technology on modern art.
    – Include photography (Cubism’s abstraction), industrial paints (Impressionism), printmaking and media (Pop Art).
